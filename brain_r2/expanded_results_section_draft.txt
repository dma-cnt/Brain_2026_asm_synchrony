Results

Analysis cohorts

The study began with 80 consecutive patients with drug-resistant epilepsy who underwent intracranial EEG monitoring. The analyses then used prespecified subsets according to the amount and structure of usable data for each question. The patient-level exposure-state analysis required at least 1 hour of analyzable data during both high and low medication exposure after excluding windows within 12 hours before or after seizures; 45 patients met these criteria. The continuous longitudinal analyses required fewer than 10 seizures and at least 5 hours between successive seizures to reduce overlap between peri-ictal periods; this cohort included 67 patients and 23,402 repeated 10-minute observations. The 30-minute binned generalized estimating equation analysis required at least 20 complete 30-minute bins and included 59 of these patients, corresponding to 1180 binned observations. Thus, the exposure-state analysis used one paired contrast per patient, whereas the continuous analyses used repeated observations with uncertainty estimated at the patient level or with methods designed for correlated longitudinal data.

Synchrony is higher when medication load is lower

We first asked whether the association between synchrony and medication load was visible within individual patients before fitting continuous longitudinal models. In the 45 patients with sufficient data in both exposure states, median synchrony was compared during high ASM exposure, defined as ASM load >=80%, and low ASM exposure, defined as ASM load <=50%, after excluding peri-ictal windows (Fig. 2A). Synchrony was higher during low medication exposure in 32 of 45 patients. The median paired difference, calculated as low-exposure synchrony minus high-exposure synchrony, was 0.0082 on the bounded 0-1 synchrony scale. Expressed relative to each patient's high-exposure state, this corresponded to a median 7.4% increase in synchrony during low exposure.

This effect was small in absolute synchrony units but consistent at the patient level. The paired contrast was supported by an exact sign test (P = 0.007) and a Wilcoxon signed-rank test (P = 0.0004), with a bootstrap 95% confidence interval for the median paired difference of 0.0036 to 0.0143 (Fig. 2B). Because each patient contributed one paired difference, this analysis did not rely on treating repeated 10-minute windows as independent observations. Sensitivity analyses showed the same direction of effect when the peri-ictal exclusion window was shortened to 1 hour, when no seizure exclusion was applied and when alternative high- and low-exposure thresholds were used (Fig. 2C). These analyses indicate that higher synchrony during lower medication exposure was not explained by one specific threshold choice or by the most conservative seizure-exclusion rule.

Synchrony continuously tracks medication load

The exposure-state analysis tested a clinically interpretable contrast between high and low medication states. We next tested whether synchrony was associated with continuous ASM load across the monitoring admission. The continuous analysis included 67 patients and 23,402 repeated 10-minute patient-window observations, with adjustment for cumulative time since admission, time since previous seizure, time until next seizure, alpha-delta ratio, time-of-day terms and interictal spike rate. Continuous predictors were standardized before fitting; therefore, synchrony coefficients describe the expected change in modelled ASM load per 1 standard deviation increase in synchrony.

Patient-clustered GEE

In the primary patient-clustered generalized estimating equation, higher synchrony was associated with lower ASM load (beta = -0.153, robust standard error = 0.072, 95% CI -0.293 to -0.012, P = 0.033; Fig. 3B; Table 1). The working correlation was specified as independent, but inference used robust sandwich standard errors with patient as the clustering unit. This analysis therefore estimated a population-average association while calculating uncertainty across patients rather than across individual time windows.

Circular-shift analysis

We next asked whether this association depended on the original timing of synchrony relative to the medication-load curve. In each of 5000 permutations, the synchrony time series was circularly shifted within each patient, preserving the distribution and temporal autocorrelation of synchrony while disrupting its alignment with ASM load and other time-varying covariates. The shifted-null distribution was not centred at zero; its median coefficient was beta = -0.136, consistent with broad temporal structure during the admission. The observed patient-clustered GEE coefficient was beta = -0.153 and lay farther from the shifted-null median than expected under this dependence-preserving null. Eighty-eight of 5000 shifted datasets were at least as far from the shifted-null median as the observed dataset, giving a median-centred two-sided empirical P = 0.018. Thus, the observed alignment between synchrony and ASM load exceeded what would be expected from temporally structured synchrony fluctuations alone.

Thirty-minute binned GEE

The association also persisted after reducing the temporal density of the data. After averaging observations into non-overlapping 30-minute bins and fitting a generalized estimating equation with an unstructured within-patient working correlation matrix, higher synchrony again corresponded to lower ASM load in 59 patients and 1180 binned observations (beta = -0.028, robust standard error = 0.010, 95% CI -0.048 to -0.008, P = 0.007; Fig. 3B; Table 1). This analysis addresses the concern that the result depends on densely sampled adjacent 10-minute windows, because the association remained evident after coarser temporal aggregation and with a flexible within-patient correlation structure.

Mixed-effects and likelihood tests

In the mixed-effects model with patient-specific random intercepts, higher synchrony was also associated with lower ASM load after adjustment for clinical and physiological covariates (beta = -0.285, standard error = 0.016, 95% CI -0.316 to -0.254, P < 0.001; Fig. 3A). Cumulative time since EMU admission and seizure-timing variables were associated with ASM load, as expected because medication tapering is structured by the clinical course of the admission. Interictal spike rate was not independently associated with ASM load in this model (beta = -0.005, standard error = 0.005, P = 0.34), indicating that the synchrony association was not simply a surrogate for interictal epileptiform activity. Adding synchrony improved model fit beyond the other covariates in the random-intercept model (likelihood ratio = 328.30, P < 0.001) and in the model allowing patient-specific synchrony slopes (likelihood ratio = 9.57, P = 0.002). In the random-slope model, the fixed synchrony effect remained negative (beta = -0.422, 95% CI -0.680 to -0.164, P = 0.001; Fig. 3B).

Continuous-analysis summary

Across complementary continuous analyses, higher synchrony was associated with lower ASM load. The patient-clustered generalized estimating equation estimated uncertainty at the patient level, the circular-shift analysis showed that the observed timing exceeded a dependence-preserving null, the 30-minute binned generalized estimating equation reduced reliance on dense temporal sampling and the likelihood-ratio tests showed that synchrony improved model fit beyond clinical and physiological covariates (Table 1). These findings support synchrony as a candidate physiological index of medication-related network state while recognizing that medication load in the EMU is also strongly structured by the clinical timing of taper and re-initiation.

Electrode subsampling preserves temporal dynamics of synchrony

Finally, we asked whether relative synchrony dynamics were preserved when synchrony was calculated from fewer electrodes. Using fewer electrodes increased absolute synchrony values (Fig. 4A), an expected consequence of applying the Kuramoto order parameter to fewer oscillators. However, relative temporal dynamics were preserved. The median Pearson correlation between subsampled synchrony and synchrony computed from the full electrode set increased as the proportion of sampled electrodes increased (Fig. 4B). The largest gain occurred between 10% and 20% sampling. At 40% sampling, subsampled synchrony was strongly correlated with full-array synchrony (median r = 0.85, IQR 0.63-0.90). This result supports preservation of relative synchrony fluctuations under reduced electrode sampling in the EMU data, although it does not establish equivalence of absolute synchrony values or validate outpatient use.
